# Domain 16: Presenting Data for Decisions

*This is a taster copy of one domain from a maintained private rules pack (16 domains, 260 rules, each with a cited source and a review cadence), exported as a point-in-time snapshot. The maintained original, its revision history and its proving records live in the private repository; open an issue on this repository to ask about access.*

**Trigger:** you are about to put a number, a chart or a table in front of somebody who has to decide something: reaching for a chart type, collapsing rows into one figure, setting axes, colours or a legend, laying out a dashboard, or writing the title above a chart.

**Load this when:** you are about to put a number, a chart or a table in front of somebody who has
to decide something. That covers the moment you open a plotting library or a spreadsheet and reach
for a chart type; the moment you collapse a column of thousands of rows into one figure for a
slide; the moment you decide whether the y-axis starts at zero and which years the x-axis covers;
the moment you choose colours for a series, or reach for a legend; the moment somebody asks for a
dashboard and you start laying out tiles; the moment you write the title above the chart, which is
the sentence most readers will remember and the only one some of them will read; the moment you
are about to say "sales are up 12%" without saying up from what, over what period, or how much
that number moves on its own; and the moment you are tempted to add a second y-axis so two series
will fit on one picture.

This domain is about the artefact that carries the numbers and the summary statistics inside it.
Four neighbouring subjects are deliberately not repeated here. The interface around the chart, the
forms, the flows, the button labels, the error messages and the prototyping that de-risks them, is
domain 15; nothing below is about the screen the chart sits on. Working out what the
decision-maker actually needs, eliciting and validating that as a requirement, is domain 02; this
domain assumes somebody has already told you roughly what question is being asked and picks up
where you start answering it visually. Modelling and storing the underlying data is domain 01.
Getting the data in the first place, and cleaning it, is covered nowhere in this framework at
present and is recorded in Known gaps: every rule below assumes the numbers you are presenting are
already correct, and none of them will save you if they are not.

## A. Before you chart: what decision, whose question, is a chart even needed

### 1. Write the message as a sentence before you choose a chart type; if you cannot, the problem is not the chart.

The default failure is opening the charting tool first and asking what it means afterwards, which
produces a picture of a dataset rather than an answer to a question. The symptom is a chart whose
caption is a restatement of its axes ("revenue by region, 2021 to 2025") because nobody could say
what the reader is supposed to conclude. Forcing the message into a sentence first catches the two
cases that no amount of formatting will fix: the chart carrying three messages at once, which
should be three charts, and the chart carrying none, which should be deleted. This is also the
cheapest point at which to discover the honest answer is "the data does not say".

*Source: UK Government Analysis Function, "Data visualisation: charts," analysisfunction.civilservice.gov.uk/policy-store/data-visualisation-charts/ (fetched and read this session; page states "Publication date: 19 May 2022"), section "Choosing the right chart": "Think about the message your chart is aiming to get across. A good way of thinking about this is to imagine you are describing what the chart is showing over the phone" and, as the check: "If you cannot write down the message your chart is giving in a few sentences, you should think again about the chart you have chosen." The same section supplies the multiple-message test: "Ask yourself: Can I simplify this? Can I break it down in another way? Am I trying to communicate too many messages within one chart?" Office for National Statistics Service Manual, "Principles," service-manual.ons.gov.uk/data-visualisation/guidance/principles (fetched and read this session; no last-updated date was shown on the page): "Plotting values on a chart alone does not guarantee the trends will be clear to the user." Nielsen Norman Group, Kate Moran, "Choosing Chart Types: Consider Context," 30 January 2022, nngroup.com/articles/choosing-chart-types/ (fetched and read this session) states the same discipline as the first step: "Consider one key question before you begin designing your chart: What is your goal?" and "In other words, what point are you trying to make? What do you want your audience to understand when they look at your chart? What action do you want them to take?" The specific observation that a caption restating the axes is the tell of a message-free chart is common practice; checked the Analysis Function charts guidance and the ONS principles page, 2026-08-06, nothing on point. Rule id: D16-R01. Volatility: durable. Verified: 2026-08-06 (current, publisher documentation, for the Analysis Function and ONS guidance; current, primary source, for the NN/g article).*

### 2. Choose between a table, a chart and a plain sentence by what the reader has to do with the number.

A chart is not the default and it is not free. If the reader needs to look up or compare exact
values, read figures of very different magnitudes, or take totals and percentages away with them,
a table does that better and a chart forces them to estimate from pixels. If the reader needs to
see a shape, a trend or a relationship, a table hides it and a chart shows it. If the answer is a
single figure and one comparison, a sentence beats both and costs nothing to make accessible. The
failure this prevents is the chart built because charts look analytical, which turns a
one-sentence answer into a picture the reader has to decode before they can act on it, and the
opposite failure of a wall of digits where the whole point was a trend.

*Source: Government Analysis Function, "Data visualisation: tables," analysisfunction.civilservice.gov.uk/policy-store/data-visualisation-tables/ (fetched and read this session; page states "Publication date: 19 May 2022"), which divides the work explicitly. "Demonstration tables are useful when:" is followed by "you want users to compare values", "you want to include values and measures such as percentages or indices", "you want to include summary statistics such as averages or totals" and "you need to show values of very different sizes in the same display, for example values in the tens and values in the millions". Against that: "Charts should be used when you want to show patterns, trends and relationships," and "Reference tables should be used when you want to supply large amounts of data for further analysis." The Analysis Function charts guidance (as cited at rule 1, fetched and read this session) makes the same point from the chart side, on value labels: "And, if in doubt, remember that a well structured table can often be as powerful as a chart." ONS Service Manual, "Principles" (as cited at rule 1, fetched and read this session): "Avoid using charts or maps to show information that is unimportant, or supplementary to the main findings," and "Only include the data that are needed to show the important trends clearly and accurately. Data visualisations should not be used to share your entire dataset." The claim that a single figure plus one comparison is better served by a sentence than by either a chart or a table is now sourced to the field's reference text: Few, *Show Me the Numbers: Designing Tables and Graphs to Enlighten*, 2nd edition (Analytics Press, 2012; held copy, string-matched this cycle), p. 39: "When the quantitative information you want to convey consists only of a single number or two, written language is an effective means of communication; your message can be expressed simply as a sentence or highlighted as a bullet point. If your message is that last quarter's sales totaled $1,485,393 and exceeded the forecast by 16%, then it isn't necessary to structure the message as a table, and there is certainly no need to create a graph." That is this rule's single-figure-plus-one-comparison case stated with its own worked example. Rule id: D16-R02. Volatility: durable. Verified: 2026-08-06 (current, publisher documentation); 2026-08-08 (current, print primary, held copy, string-matched, p. 39, for the sentence-over-table-or-chart claim).*

### 3. Ship every number with the comparison that makes it mean something.

A bare figure is uninterpretable and the reader will supply a comparison whether or not you give
them one, usually the wrong one. "Conversion is 24%" supports no decision at all until you say
what it was last quarter, what the other product line does, or what the target was. The concrete
failure is a stakeholder acting on a number that is entirely normal because nothing on the page
told them it was normal, or failing to act on one that is unprecedented for the same reason. The
comparison is a design decision, not decoration: which baseline you pick determines what the
chart appears to say, so pick it for relevance to the decision and state which one you picked.
Sometimes the honest presentation is not the raw values at all but the derived variable the
reader actually cares about, the change, the difference from average, or the rate.

*Source: NN/g, "Choosing Chart Types" (as cited at rule 1, fetched and read this session): "Numbers are meaningless without context. If I told you that the completion rate for checking out in an ecommerce app was 24%, what would that mean? Nothing, unless I also told you that the completion rate had been 17% in the previous year. People need to compare a number against another number in order to interpret it." The article also makes comparison a layout decision: "What comparison do you want people to make? Put those things close together, so your viewers easily see them side by side and understand your main point." Government Analysis Function, "Writing about data," analysisfunction.civilservice.gov.uk/policy-store/writing-about-data/ (fetched and read in full on 2026-08-07; page states "Publication date:" "3 April 2025"), the live successor to the guidance this publisher archived in April 2025, gives the rule a section of its own, "Put the data into context", whose instructions are to "explain the overall trend and highlight specific areas of interest", to "help the audience understand the importance of the data" and to "make comparisons between groups to show important differences", closing with "highlight any implications of the data". It carries the archived page's worked instance forward, now put as a question: "for example, is a 2% rise unusual compared to previous years, other countries, or the policy target?" The predecessor, Government Analysis Function, "Writing about statistics," analysisfunction.civilservice.gov.uk/policy-store/writing-about-statistics/ (re-fetched and re-read on 2026-08-07; page states "Publication date:" "27 November 2019" and "Updates:" "25 September 2020 - Updates related to accessibility and publishing in HTML", and now carries the notice "This item is archived. Information presented here may be out of date." above a link naming "Writing about data" as the April 2025 replacement), is retained here for the one claim the successor drops, which is the warning against the isolated comparison: "Don’t focus solely on the latest numbers, or on point-to-point comparisons in isolation. Instead, give the overall picture, drawing attention to individual movements only where they add value to the story." The two quotes this rule previously led on, "Do not report on changes without discussing the context," and "For example, if you report a 2% rise, help the user understand whether this is typical or unusual in comparison to previous statistics, to other countries, or in respect to policy targets," are superseded by the live quotes above and are no longer what the rule rests on. On derived variables, ONS Service Manual, "Principles" (as cited at rule 1) advises that "In some cases, the most effective way to convey the trends in your data is to use a derived variable," naming "percentage point change", "difference from an average", "percentage of a total" and "rate per person". Rule id: D16-R03. Volatility: durable. Verified: 2026-08-06 (current, primary source, for the NN/g article; current, publisher documentation, for the ONS guidance); 2026-08-07 (current, publisher documentation, for the Analysis Function "Writing about data" quotes; named, publisher-archived documentation fetched live 2026-08-07, for the retained "Writing about statistics" quote, whose claim the 3 April 2025 successor does not carry).*

## B. Choosing the encoding: what the picture is actually asking the eye to do

### 4. Pick the chart from the relationship you are showing, and prefer the simple familiar type.

Chart choice is not taste, it is a lookup from the statistical relationship to the encodings that
represent it faithfully. Time series to lines, ranking and magnitude to bars, correlation to
scatterplots, part-to-whole to bars or a tightly constrained pie, distribution to bars or a
population pyramid. The failure mode this prevents is reaching for whatever the tool offered
first: multiple time series drawn as clustered bars, where the eye cannot follow a series across
clusters, or a novel chart type chosen because it looks sophisticated, which spends the reader's
attention on decoding the format rather than the finding. The second half of the rule is the
harder discipline: when one chart is being asked to show two relationships at once, two simple
charts almost always beat one clever one.

*Source: Analysis Function, "Data visualisation: charts" (as cited at rule 1, fetched and read this session), which supplies an explicit mapping table under "Statistical relationships": Distribution to "Bar chart, population pyramid, box plot, dot plot"; Time series to "Line chart, calendar heat map"; Ranking to "Bar chart, lollipop chart, slope chart"; Deviation to "Bar chart, dot plot"; Correlation to "Scatterplot, line graph"; Magnitude to "Bar chart"; Spatial to "Map"; Part-to-whole to "Bar chart, pie chart, donut chart, tree map, bubble chart"; Flow to "Sankey graph". The page introduces it with "After you have decided on the message, consider the statistical relationship you’re trying to show." On the clustered-bars failure it is explicit: "when you have more than one time series on a chart, do not use a bar chart, use a line chart instead," and "Line charts are better for displaying time series data when there is more than one series." ONS Service Manual, "Choosing a chart type," service-manual.ons.gov.uk/data-visualisation/chart-types/choosing-a-chart-type (fetched and read this session; no last-updated date was shown on the page) names eight relationships (Correlation, Deviation, Distribution, Geography, Magnitude, Parts of a whole, Ranking and Time) and states the preference for the familiar: "Use simple charts that will be familiar to users whenever possible. Complex or unfamiliar charts can be difficult to interpret and may obscure the relationships you are trying to display." On the two-charts point: "Charts showing multiple relationships are often less clear than charts focused on a single relationship," and "two simple charts can be more effective than one complex chart." NN/g, "Choosing Chart Types" (as cited at rule 1) narrows the default set further: "Researchers have found that bar charts, line charts, and scatter plots are easiest to understand." Rule id: D16-R04. Volatility: durable. Verified: 2026-08-06 (current, publisher documentation, for the Analysis Function and ONS mappings; current, primary source, for the NN/g article).*

### 5. Encode quantity as length or 2D position; treat area, angle, 3D and dual axes as encodings that mislead.

This is the perceptual core of the domain and the reason chart choice is not arbitrary. People
compare lengths and positions accurately and pre-attentively; they compare areas and angles badly,
noticing that one is bigger without being able to say by how much. So a pie chart, a donut, a
gauge, a treemap and a bubble chart all ask the eye to do the thing it is worst at, and a 3D
effect additionally distorts the encoding that would otherwise have worked. Dual axes are a
separate and worse case, because the misreading is not imprecision but invention: the two vertical
scales are chosen by the author, and the same two series can be made to cross, converge or diverge
at will. The failure this prevents is a decision taken on an apparent relationship that exists
only in the choice of scales, and the everyday version where a reader concludes one slice is twice
another when it is not.

*Source: Nielsen Norman Group, Page Laubheimer, "Dashboards: Making Charts and Graphs Easier to Understand," 18 June 2017, nngroup.com/articles/dashboards-preattentive/ (fetched and read this session): "we are quite adept at estimating how lengths compare, and we can also accurately estimate position in a 2D space," against "although we can effortlessly notice a large rectangle in a display containing many small rectangles, we are not good at judging how much bigger the large rectangle is, compared with the others." On the circular family: "Circular graphs like pie charts, gauges, and radar charts do not convey well quantitative relationships between data, as they rely on area and angle to communicate quantitative information," and "Angle, too, communicates quantitative information poorly", another sentence that continues past the point quoted. The article's verdict on pies is direct: "Pie charts (and their even less helpful cousin, donut charts) are notoriously poor at most information-communication tasks", a sentence that continues past the point quoted. On 3D, it states that plotting a two-variable chart in 3D "will make the chart much more difficult to interpret quickly and accurately, as it distorts the shapes and alignment of the visual features showing data," and "In a 3D visualization, it becomes much tougher to determine which bar is shortest, even though length is a very efficient preattentive variable when presented in 2D." NN/g, Kate Moran, "Clutter-Free: One of the 3 Cs for Better Charts," 6 February 2022, nngroup.com/articles/clutter-charts/ (fetched and read this session) is unconditional: "While nobody should ever use 3D designs in their data visualizations, other design elements can be informational or distracting depending on the context," and warns that decorative chart styles "can even create an optical illusion, distorting users’ perceptions of the data values." NN/g, "Choosing Chart Types" (as cited at rule 1) generalises the point: "Other chart types require users to visually assess angle, area, or volume to understand the data," listing pie, bubble, mosaic, unit or waffle, Sankey or dendrogram, and stacked area charts. On dual axes, Analysis Function, "Data visualisation: charts" (as cited at rule 1): "In general, we do not recommend using dual axis charts because:" is followed by "they can be easily misinterpreted" and "the way we display lines in relation to each other can manipulate the data story, even if it is not intentional", with "Dual axis line charts can be manipulated to show almost any relationship," and the remedy "It is best practice to separate the datasets and present them with their own y-axis." ONS Service Manual, "Axes and gridlines," service-manual.ons.gov.uk/data-visualisation/guidance/axes-and-gridlines (fetched and read this session; no last-updated date was shown on the page): "Avoid using dual axis charts as they are often misleading and difficult to interpret. Instead, use either two charts or one chart with a single axis and annotations to explain the trends." Where a pie is genuinely used, the Analysis Function bounds it. "Pie charts should only be used when:" is followed by "there are five categories or fewer", "the categories sum to a meaningful whole (you can combine categories when appropriate, but never remove a category from the ‘whole’)" and "there is a dominant category (if several categories are a similar size, use a bar chart instead)". Rule id: D16-R05. Volatility: durable. Verified: 2026-08-06 (current, primary source, for the perceptual claims; current, publisher documentation, for the dual-axis and pie constraints).*

### 6. Label the series directly, and delete everything in the picture that is not carrying data.

Two habits account for most of the noise in a working chart. The first is the legend, which is the
default output of every charting tool and which forces the reader to hold a colour in memory,
travel to the key, and travel back, once per series; it is also the most common way a chart ends
up conveying its meaning through colour alone. Direct labels on the line, the bar or the sector
remove the lookup and the colour dependency in one move. The second is the furniture: shaded
backgrounds, boxes, heavy gridlines, shadows, textures, redundant markers and gridlines that
duplicate data labels already on the bars. Each one competes with the data for attention and none
of it is load-bearing. The failure this prevents is a reader who takes longer to find the finding
than to act on it, and gives up somewhere in between.

*Source: NN/g, "Clutter-Free" (as cited at rule 5, fetched and read this session): "The problem with legends is that they require people to quickly look back and forth between the data visualization and the legend. It’s easier for people to process these series if we place the meaningful labels directly on each line," and the accessibility reason, "In addition to being easier to process, this approach is also more accessible." and "Placing the labels directly above each line eliminates that problem." Its closing instruction is the whole rule: "Remove any unnecessary visual elements that distract from your meaning." Analysis Function, "Data visualisation: charts" (as cited at rule 1) gives the same two halves. On legends: "Avoiding using legends unless necessary. This is because legends use colour to match labels to data," and "In line charts legends can be avoided by labelling the lines directly." For pies: "Do not use a key, label the categories themselves. This avoids users having to use colour to match the label to the data." On furniture, under "Keep it simple," the page says to avoid "shaded backgrounds", "unnecessary borders", "boxes around legends and other content", "patterns, textures and shadows", "3D shapes", "unnecessary data markers on line charts" and "thick or dark gridlines", and on gridline count, "Use the right amount of gridlines" with the advice that too many clutters the chart and too few are not useful. ONS Service Manual, "Axes and gridlines" (as cited at rule 5): "Do not use too many gridlines, as this can make the chart cluttered," with the working range "In most cases, use 6 to 10 gridlines for a standard desktop view" and "use three to six gridlines for a mobile view." Government Analysis Function, "Data visualisation: colours," analysisfunction.civilservice.gov.uk/policy-store/data-visualisation-colours-in-charts/ (fetched and read this session; page states "Publication date: 23 November 2021" and "Updates: 12 February 2026 - sequential colour palette guidance updated") adds the restraint on colour itself: "Limiting the number of colours you use in a chart makes them stand out," and "When people look at a chart with lots of colours, they try to work out what those different colours mean. If your colours suggest meanings that are not there the user will waste time and effort trying to understand them." Rule id: D16-R06. Volatility: durable. Verified: 2026-08-06 (current, primary source, for the NN/g article; current, publisher documentation, for the Analysis Function and ONS guidance).*

## C. Integrity: the choices that change what the picture says

### 7. Start a bar at zero. Crop an axis only where nothing is filled to it, and mark the crop.

A bar communicates through its length, so the reader reads the ratio of two bars as the ratio of
two values. Move the baseline off zero and that ratio becomes an artefact of where you put the
axis: a fall from 11.1 to 10.1 can be drawn to look like a collapse. This is the single most
common way an honest analyst produces a dishonest picture, and it has been measured, not merely
asserted. The permission and the prohibition are both specific: line charts and scatterplots do
not fill to the axis, so cropping them is legitimate and often necessary to show the trend at all,
but the crop must be visibly marked, must break at a round number, and must be mentioned in the
text description, because an unmarked crop invites a reader to compare it against a chart that
does not have one.

*Source: Analysis Function, "Data visualisation: charts" (as cited at rule 1, fetched and read this session), section "Do not break the numerical axis on bar charts": "Breaking the numerical axis is when you make the axis start from a number other than zero." The page continues that this can be a way to zoom into the data but "it is not always appropriate," followed by "breaking the numerical axis on a bar chart is problematic and highly controversial" and the perceptual reason, "In bar charts you perceive the bars being proportional to each other". The remedy offered is a different chart, not a different axis: "If starting the numerical axis at zero stops you from telling the story clearly, consider an alternative chart". For lines, the same page permits the crop and constrains it: "it is acceptable to break a numerical y-axis on a line chart, when necessary. Line charts are not read in the same way as bar charts so breaking the numerical axis does not mislead in the same way," listing the disadvantages "It can exaggerate trends and emphasise very small differences between data points"; "There is a risk that users may miss the break or not understand the symbol used to indicate the break"; "There is a risk that users may compare graphs which are not equivalent if one has a broken y-axis and the other does not." Its formatting requirements include "ensure the break is from a rounded number" and "mention the break in the text description of the chart (necessary for accessibility)". ONS Service Manual, "Axes and gridlines" (as cited at rule 5) states the same split as a rule of construction: "For charts that use a filled area or line starting from the axis to represent data values, the axis should always start at zero. This includes bar charts and area charts," and "For charts where the data points are not connected to the axis, crop the axis if needed. This includes line charts and scatter plots." Datawrapper Academy, "Why our column and bar charts start at zero (or below)," datawrapper.de/academy/why-our-column-and-bar-charts-start-at-zero (fetched and read this session; page states "Last updated on March 23rd, 2021") supplies the empirical support and a product that enforces it: "Truncated y-axes in column charts and bar charts are considered deceptive and misleading. And there's research backing that up," citing Pandey, Nov, Rall, Bertini and Satterthwaite's 2015 paper "How Deceptive are Deceptive Visualizations?" for the findings that "participants who saw the deceptive visualization perceived the underlying message in its exaggerated form" and that such techniques "do lead to major misinterpretation from the reader’s side and that the effects are also rather large." The page states the product consequence, "That's why Datawrapper doesn't allow you to begin your y-axis at something greater than zero," and the alternative, "Readers don't expect dot plots (or, for two values, range plots) to start at zero because there's no filled bar or column that would indicate that." The Pandey et al. paper itself was not fetched or read; it is cited here only as quoted by Datawrapper. Rule id: D16-R07. Volatility: fast (the claim about a specific product refusing truncated axes moves with releases; the perceptual rule and the government guidance behind it do not). Verified: 2026-08-06 (current, publisher documentation, all three sources).*

### 8. Hold the scale, the window and the line continuous only where the data is; never imply a comparison the data does not support.

Three separate manipulations share one shape, which is a picture that implies a comparison the
underlying numbers do not license. The first is inconsistent scales across charts a reader will
compare side by side, including small multiples, where different y-axes make unequal things look
equal. The second is the chosen window: starting a time series at the trough, or ending it before
the reversal, is a real decision made silently, and the aspect ratio does the same job more subtly
by flattening or steepening every slope in the chart. The third is drawing a line across a gap in
the data, which asserts values that were never measured, or across a methodology change, which
asserts that two different measurements are the same measurement. That third one has a quieter
form that is easy to miss because nothing is drawn at all: a period or a category with no events
dropped from a table or a series rather than shown as a zero, so the reader meets a gap in the
dates or a missing category and cannot tell a genuinely quiet period from one nothing was recorded
for. Absence of measurement and a measurement of zero are different facts and must look different.
Each of these produces a reader who is confident about something false, which is worse than a
reader who is uncertain.

*Source: ONS Service Manual, "Axes and gridlines" (as cited at rule 5, fetched and read this session): "If you are making several charts that show comparable data, try to use the same axis scale for each chart. Different scales can be misleading. Consistent scales help users understand the relative size of values." Analysis Function, "Data visualisation: charts" (as cited at rule 1, fetched and read this session) says the same of small multiples: "it is essential for all the y-axes to have the same scale to avoid misunderstandings". On aspect ratio: "In line charts, the aspect ratio you choose alters the slope of the lines. This can be misleading," with the quoted advice to choose one that makes a line chart "appear reasonably neutral, without either making the changes appear too extreme (by picking an aspect ratio that is too small) or too flat (with an aspect ratio that is too high)". On discontinuities, the same page: "Breaks in time series can occur due to missing data points and changes in methods," and "The best way to deal with discontinuity varies depending on the data and the story you are telling. But, it must always be highlighted," with the concrete instruction "If you do use a line, do not join the points either side of the missing data point, even if the line is dotted or dashed. Joining points implies we know something about the data." Analysis Function, "Writing about statistics" (the archived predecessor page, as cited at rule 3, re-fetched and re-read on 2026-08-07) covers the window from the commentary side: "Don’t focus solely on the latest numbers, or on point-to-point comparisons in isolation." Its live successor, Analysis Function, "Writing about data" (as cited at rules 3 and 20, fetched and read in full on 2026-08-07), does not repeat that prohibition. The nearest it comes is the instruction to "explain the overall trend and highlight specific areas of interest", which asks for the whole picture but says nothing about the latest figure or about the isolated pair, so it is not quoted here as support; the archived page remains the publisher authority for this claim's precise form and is retained on that basis; the nearest current authority is the Code of Practice for Statistics, Edition 3.0 (as cited at rule 11, downloaded and read in full 2026-08-07), whose standards for the public use of statistics require "Do not use statistics, data or wider analysis in a misleading way. This includes not cherry-picking figures, taking figures out of context or placing undue certainty on them", which prohibits the selective isolated figure without stating the point-to-point formulation. The specific claim that starting or ending a series at a chosen point is itself a silent editorial decision is common practice; checked the Analysis Function charts guidance, the Analysis Function writing guidance and the ONS axes guidance, 2026-08-06, and the Analysis Function "Writing about data" successor guidance, 2026-08-07, and none states it in those terms. The omitted row and the omitted category, added on 2026-08-09, are sourced to the field's reference texts. Few, *Show Me the Numbers: Designing Tables and Graphs to Enlighten*, 2nd edition (Analytics Press, 2012; held copy, string-matched this cycle), p. 261, states the requirement for a series of small multiples: "Make sure that the categorical scale also remains consistent with the same items in the same order (e.g., always West, South, East, and North) and the same full set of items in each graph even when a value is zero or null." His worked failure is this defect exactly: "Rather than displaying a value of zero, the south region was excluded from the graph entirely, resulting in a confusing inconsistency", and the accompanying figure caption names what the reader is left holding, "the south region is missing in the middle graph of distributor sales." Nussbaumer Knaflic, *Storytelling with Data: A Data Visualization Guide for Business Professionals* (Wiley, 2015; held copy, string-matched this cycle), p. 46, states the time-axis form of the same requirement: "Note that when you're graphing time on the horizontal x-axis of a line graph, the data plotted must be in consistent intervals." and closes the point with "Be consistent in the time points you plot." (Knaflic's text sets that compound with a Unicode hyphen character rather than an ASCII one, and uses a curly apostrophe in "you're"; both are reproduced here with their plain equivalents, and the span was matched under that normalisation.) ### 9. Show uncertainty where it would change the decision, in plain words, and never on a chart that cannot carry it.

Uncertainty is not a footnote and it is not decoration; it is the difference between "these two
groups differ" and "we cannot tell whether these two groups differ", which are opposite inputs to
a decision. The judgement is a real one in both directions. Ranges drawn on every series when they
are narrow and non-overlapping add clutter that buys nothing. Ranges omitted when they overlap
heavily let a reader conclude a difference exists when the data does not support it, which is the
expensive failure. Three structural traps follow. Some chart types cannot show uncertainty at all,
so choosing them is a decision to hide it. Where uncertainty is so wide that no comparison
holds, the correct output is not a hedged chart but no chart. And a pipeline can compute
uncertainty and then simply fail to carry it: a confidence figure calculated for every record and
shown to whoever operates the system, then dropped on the way into the reader's artefact, leaves
every value on the page looking equally certain, which is a claim the upstream data never made.
Uncertainty that exists and is not carried forward is uncertainty hidden, and it is hidden by
omission rather than by choice, which is why nobody notices.

*Source: ONS Service Manual, "Showing uncertainty in charts," service-manual.ons.gov.uk/data-visualisation/guidance/showing-uncertainty-in-charts (fetched and read this session; no last-updated date was shown on the page), which frames the trade-off directly: "In some cases, showing uncertainty is important to give an accurate impression of the data. However, it also adds complexity that makes charts harder to read," and names the structural trap, "Some chart types, such as stacked bar charts, cannot effectively show uncertainty." On both directions of the judgement: "You should not show uncertainty on charts if it is not necessary for interpreting the data," where the ranges are consistently small or clearly separated, against "You should show uncertainty when it is important for understanding key trends in the data and when it would fundamentally change the interpretation," specifically "if there are large overlaps between uncertainty ranges for different categories, suggesting comparisons may not be statistically valid." The page also gives the stop condition: "If uncertainty is so high that no meaningful trends or comparisons can be made, do not show the data in a chart." On presentation: "We do not use bar or column charts with error bars, as they are harder to interpret accurately," and on wording, "Use plain language, such as, “we are 95% sure the value falls in this range”," with the instruction to "Avoid technical terms like “confidence interval” or “statistical significance,” or, if they are needed, provide simple explanations." Government Analysis Function (Data Quality Hub), "Communicating quality, uncertainty and change," analysisfunction.civilservice.gov.uk/policy-store/communicating-quality-uncertainty-and-change/ (fetched and read this session; page states "Publication date: 17 December 2018" and carries the notice "This page is currently under review and awaiting updates," and "this guidance has not yet been updated to incorporate the accessibility legislation"): "Uncertainty helps us to describe the limitations of our statistics. ### 10. Title the chart with the finding, and make sure the finding is the thing the picture actually shows.

The title is the highest-leverage text in the artefact, because it is read by everyone including
the people who will not study the chart, and it is quoted onward. A descriptive title ("success
rates by task") makes the reader do the analysis you were paid to do. A title that states the
finding does the work for them, and it has a second effect that matters more: writing it forces
you to check that the finding is visible. If the title says one thing and the strongest visual
pattern says another, the chart is wrong, not the title. The boundary this rule polices is that
emphasising a real finding is legitimate and manufacturing one is not; the same tools, colour,
annotation and title, do both, and the difference is whether the emphasis survives the data
disagreeing with you. All of that assumes a person writes the sentence while looking at the
picture. When the artefact is generated, nobody is there at the moment of writing, so a finding
sentence hard-coded into the template is a verdict written before the data existed and it ships
whether the data supports it or not. Any generated finding sentence must therefore be conditional
on the figures that would support it, and where no condition holds the correct output is the
descriptive line, not the confident one.

*Source: NN/g, Kate Moran, "Contrast: One of the 3Cs for Better Charts," 13 February 2022, nngroup.com/articles/contrast-charts/ (fetched and read this session): "Use a meaningful title that tells the audience the finding or takeaway. By far, an informative title is the easiest big-impact change that you can make in your chart," and "Titles should not merely describe the data shown", followed by "Don’t force viewers to search for the takeaway; Draw the conclusion for your audience." The article's worked pairs contrast the passive "Login rates before and after redesign" with the active "Login rates improved by 29% after redesign". On the boundary: "Designing a chart to emphasize a finding is not the same as manipulating a chart to make it misleading," and "Don’t try to obscure findings that contradict your expectations or do not serve your political goals in the organization." ONS Service Manual, "Chart text," service-manual.ons.gov.uk/data-visualisation/guidance/chart-text (fetched and read this session; no last-updated date was shown on the page) states both halves as requirements: "The chart title should describe the main trend you want the chart to show to the user," with the evidence claim "Research shows that users are more likely to understand and remember the main trend from a chart where it is also in the chart title," and the consistency check that this rule turns on, "Your chart may not be effective if the trend described in your chart title is not the most prominent visual trend in the chart. ## D. Summary statistics inside the artefact

This section covers the number itself, not the picture around it. Its first three rules are about
the language and the precision you wrap a figure in; the three added on 2026-08-07 are about
choosing the figure in the first place, and they are sourced to the statistical-literacy and
methodology documentation of three national statistical offices. What is still not covered here is
recorded in Known gaps: the sample-against-population caveat a chart caption should carry, and the
presentation of a rate or a ratio whose denominator moves.

### 11. Say association when you mean association, and reserve causal verbs for evidence of cause.

Verb choice is where a correlation quietly becomes a cause. "Rollout drove a 12% lift" and
"the lift coincided with the rollout" describe the same chart and license entirely different
decisions, and the first one gets acted on. The failure is not usually deliberate: causal verbs
are shorter and read better, so they win by default in a slide title unless somebody is watching.
The practical check is to keep two vocabularies apart and pick from the correct one, and to state
plainly what the numbers can and cannot be used for rather than leaving the reader to infer it.

*Source: Government Analysis Function, "Writing about statistics" (the archived predecessor page, as cited at rule 3, re-fetched and re-read on 2026-08-07), which lists the vocabularies explicitly under "Be cautious if using words with specific meanings in the context of statistics": "Words which suggest causality: affect, cause, consequence, effect, impact." "Words which suggest relationship but not causality: association, correlation, corresponding, equivalent, parallel." "Words which suggest a more provisional explanation: expect, believe, think, predict, envisage, forecast." The same section warns "Take care not to misuse words like “significant”." The page also requires the limits to be stated rather than implied: "Be clear on what your statistics can and cannot be used for," and "Don’t bury important limitations in the supporting information. Avoid any implication that the statistics are free from error." Its live successor, Analysis Function, "Writing about data" (as cited at rules 3 and 20, fetched and read in full on 2026-08-07), carries none of the three vocabulary lists and no warning about "significant", so the archived page remains the only publisher authority for the core of this rule and is retained on that basis. The successor does carry the rule's second half, under "Be transparent about the strengths, limitations, and any uncertainty", where the instructions are to "include information to help your audience confidently use the data" and to "be clear about the quality of information, the methodology used, and any limitations", and where the worked example commends a publication that "clearly states up front what you can and cannot do with the data". The archived page's sharper form of that instruction, "Don’t bury important limitations in the supporting information. Avoid any implication that the statistics are free from error," has no successor equivalent and is retained beside it, though the don't-bury half now has a current authority: UK Statistics Authority, Code of Practice for Statistics, Edition 3.0 (code.statisticsauthority.gov.uk, PDF uploaded October 2025, downloaded and read in full 2026-08-07), whose Principle 7 requires that producers "prominently explain the quality of the statistics, including strengths and limitations", with practice 7.1, "Prominently communicate the quality of the statistics and the strengths and limitations that impact their use", and practice 7.3, "Prominently communicate limitations in the underlying data and explain their impact on the statistics"; prominence is the stated requirement, which is the opposite of burying, while the archived page's free-from-error phrasing appears in no current page. The vocabulary lists themselves remain archive-only: Analysis Function, "Advice for policy professionals using statistics and analysis" (3 February 2022, fetched and read 2026-08-07; the page notes its content is due for review) makes the correlation-and-causation point by worked example, "There is a big difference between showing that things are associated and that one thing causes another. ### 12. Never let a significance threshold make the decision, and never present a non-significant change as a change.

Two opposite errors both come from treating a threshold as a verdict. The first is reporting a
movement as real because it cleared a bar, when the bar says nothing about whether the effect is
large enough to matter. The second is presenting a wobble as a trend when it sits inside the range
the series moves in anyway, which is how a dashboard produces a monthly meeting about noise. The
second error is the one that recurs in operational reporting, because every regular series
produces small movements and somebody has to explain them. The honest presentation names the
typical level of variation and says whether this movement exceeds it. Hedging alone does not
count: telling a reader to be cautious without telling them what they can and cannot conclude
leaves them exactly where they started.

*Source: American Statistical Association, "American Statistical Association Releases Statement on Statistical Significance and P-Values," 7 March 2016, amstat.org/asa/files/pdfs/P-ValueStatement.pdf (fetched and read this session as a PDF; the PDF renders this headline in capitals), which states six principles verbatim: "1. P-values can indicate how incompatible the data are with a specified statistical model. 2. P-values do not measure the probability that the studied hypothesis is true, or the probability that the data were produced by random chance alone. 3. Scientific conclusions and business or policy decisions should not be based only on whether a p-value passes a specific threshold. 4. Proper inference requires full reporting and transparency. 5. A p-value, or statistical significance, does not measure the size of an effect or the importance of a result. 6. By itself, a p-value does not provide a good measure of evidence regarding a model or hypothesis." The release quotes Ron Wasserstein, the ASA's executive director: "The p-value was never intended to be a substitute for scientific reasoning," and "Well-reasoned statistical arguments contain much more than the value of a single number and whether that number exceeds an arbitrary threshold." On the second error, Analysis Function, "Communicating quality, uncertainty and change" (as cited at rule 9, fetched and read this session): "Changes between periods can fall within the margins of survey error and random variation, especially for weekly, monthly or quarterly series. If so, it is important for users to understand that the change they see may not be material." Its approaches include "Emphasise that changes within the bounds of random variation are indicative, rather than definitive, and should be looked at in the context of the broader series," and "Explain what the typical level of change is between measurement points in the series." On hedging: "Phrases like “care must be taken” and “exercise caution” are not sufficient by themselves, because they do not help the reader to understand what they can and cannot do with the numbers. Support statements like this with practical advice." The full ASA statement in The American Statistician was not read; three of its principles are quoted here from the ASA's own press release, which reproduces all six. Rule id: D16-R12. Volatility: medium (professional-body position statement and publisher guidance under review). ### 13. Round to the precision the number actually has, and label an estimate as an estimate.

Digits are a claim about accuracy. Reporting 3,546,882 when the underlying figure is a survey
estimate with a wide interval tells the reader the number is exact to the person, which is false,
and the falsehood is invisible because nothing in the presentation flags it. The same mistake in a
dashboard tile is worse, because a tile carries no room for a caveat and gets screenshotted into
decisions. Rounding is not just cosmetic tidying: it is where you decide how much precision you
are willing to defend, and it has a consequence you must handle rather than hide, which is that
rounded components stop summing to rounded totals. The word "estimate" in the surrounding text is
the cheapest available honesty about all of this. A share is the same claim wearing different
clothes. One incident out of three rendered as "33%" arrives with exactly the authority of thirty
out of a hundred and thirty, because the percentage sign hides how many observations are standing
behind it. So print the denominator next to the share, and set a floor below which you print the
counts and no percentage at all. What the floor should be is a judgement about the decision the
number serves, and it belongs in whatever computes the figure rather than in the reader's head.

*Source: Analysis Function, "Data visualisation: tables" (as cited at rule 2, fetched and read this session): "Presenting too much detail can make things harder for users. Rounding numbers makes them easier to read and remember," with the fitness-for-use test "The extent of rounding will depend on the intended use. A journalist may be happy to report that the population of the UK is 66 million, or that the population has changed from 64.1 million to 66.4 million. An analyst performing further calculations will want to work with more precise figures." The page states the consequence rather than hiding it: "Rounding does reduce precision. This usually means that the reported totals no longer equal the sum of the component parts." For within-table consistency it requires that you "use the same level of precision within each column." Analysis Function, "Writing about statistics" (the archived predecessor page, as cited at rule 3, re-fetched and re-read on 2026-08-07) states the accuracy claim directly: "Make sure that the level of numerical detail is appropriate given the precision of the numbers you are reporting. Figures with lots of detail give an impression of high accuracy that may be unwarranted," and "Users find it difficult to process long, complex numbers. For example, use “3.5 million” instead of “3,546,882”." Its live successor, Analysis Function, "Writing about data" (as cited at rules 3 and 20, fetched and read in full on 2026-08-07), states no principle about numerical precision at all. The current publisher authority for the operation is the ONS content style guide, "Rounding" (service-manual.ons.gov.uk/content/numbers/rounding, the same live site style.ons.gov.uk serves; fetched and read 2026-08-07; no date shown on the page): "Rounding numbers can make them easier to read and compare, although this must be balanced against the loss of precision. The level of rounding you use (for example, one decimal place or two significant figures) should be effective, and chosen according to the intended use. ### 19. Choose the mean or the median from the shape of the distribution, and say on the artefact which one you used.

The word "average" is not a measurement, it is a family of them, and which member you picked
changes the number and sometimes the sign of the finding. The mean uses every value, so a long
tail or a handful of extreme records drags it away from anything typical; the median is the middle
value, so it barely moves. On skewed data, which is most operational data anybody actually has,
salaries, response times, session lengths, order values, reporting the mean and calling it the
average overstates the typical case and nobody reading the slide can tell. The check has two
halves and both are cheap. Look at the distribution's shape before you collapse it, because the
shape decides which measure is honest. Then label the number "median" or "mean" on the chart, in
the table header, or in the tile, rather than "average", because a reader who cannot tell which one
you used cannot tell whether to believe it. The mean is not banned: on a roughly symmetric
distribution the two agree, and where the total matters the mean is the one that reconstructs it.

*Source: Australian Bureau of Statistics, "Measures of central tendency," abs.gov.au/statistics/understanding-statistics/statistical-terms-and-concepts/measures-central-tendency (fetched and read this session; page states "Release date and time" of "02/02/2023 12:00pm AEDT"), which defines the family: "A measure of central tendency (also referred to as measures of centre or central location) is a summary measure that attempts to describe a whole set of data with a single value that represents the middle or centre of its distribution." On the trade-off it is explicit in both directions. For the median: "The median is less affected by outliers and skewed data than the mean and is usually the preferred measure of central tendency when the distribution is not symmetrical." For the mean: "As the mean includes every value in the distribution the mean is influenced by outliers and skewed distributions," and "The mean is more sensitive to the existence of outliers than the median or mode." On skew specifically: "In a skewed distribution, the median is often a preferred measure of central tendency, as the mean is not usually in the middle of the distribution." The same page supplies the definition of the thing doing the dragging, "Outliers are extreme, or atypical data value(s) that are notably different from the rest of the data," with the reason to look, "It is important to detect outliers within a distribution, because they can alter the results of the data analysis." It also declines to ban the mean: "Despite the existence of outliers in a distribution, the mean can still be an appropriate measure of central tendency, especially if the rest of the data is normally distributed." ABS, "Measures of shape," abs.gov.au/statistics/understanding-statistics/statistical-terms-and-concepts/measures-shape (fetched and read this session; same release date) states the rule as a sequence, shape first: "The shape of the distribution can assist with identifying other descriptive statistics, such as which measure of central tendency is appropriate to use," followed by the two cases, "If the data are normally distributed, the mean, median and mode are all equal, and therefore are all appropriate measure of centre central tendency," and "If data are skewed, the median may be a more appropriate measure of central tendency." It defines the trigger: "Skewness is the tendency for the values to be more frequent around the high or low ends of the x-axis." Office for National Statistics, "Guide to interpreting Annual Survey of Hours and Earnings (ASHE) estimates," ons.gov.uk/employmentandlabourmarket/peopleinwork/earningsandworkinghours/methodologies/guidetointerpretingannualsurveyofhoursandearningsasheestimates (fetched and read this session; page states "Last revised:" "26 October 2017") is a national statistics office showing its working on exactly this choice for a headline figure: "There are several methods of calculating an average; most Annual Survey of Hours and Earnings (ASHE) analyses focus on the median, that is, the data value at which 50% of data values are above it and 50% of data values are below it." Its reason is the rule: "We use the median because the distribution of earnings is skewed, with more people earning lower salaries than higher salaries. When using the mean to calculate the average of a skewed distribution, it is highly influenced by those values at the upper end of the distribution and thus may not be truly representative of the average earnings of a typical person." Elsewhere it restates the same conclusion, "The median is less affected by a relatively small number of very high earners than the mean, and therefore gives a better indication of typical pay," and records that the choice was consequential enough to change a headline: "the coverage of employees for ASHE is greater than that of NES; for ASHE, the median replaces the mean as the headline statistic". ### 20. A central value on its own is half the summary; ship the spread with it, and show the distribution when the spread is the finding.

Two datasets can share a mean, a median and a mode and still be nothing like each other, which
means a lone central value can be simultaneously correct and useless. The failure is a reader who
plans for the middle and gets hit by the tail: an average response time of four minutes is a
different business depending on whether the slowest tenth of calls take five minutes or fifty. So
the minimum honest summary is a centre plus a measure of how far the values sit from it, and where
the spread is the actual finding, a distribution beats any pair of numbers. The interquartile range
and the percentiles are usually the right spread to pair with a median, because like the median
they are unmoved by a few extreme values; the standard deviation belongs with a mean and its size
tells you how much to trust that mean. One presentational constraint comes with them: percentile,
quartile and decile language is technical, and a reader who has to decode "the bottom income
decile" before they can act has been charged for the analyst's convenience. Write the same fact as
a plain-language proportion.

*Source: Australian Bureau of Statistics, "Measures of spread," abs.gov.au/statistics/understanding-statistics/statistical-terms-and-concepts/measures-spread (fetched and read this session; page states "Release date and time" of "02/02/2023 12:00pm AEDT"), which builds the whole rule on a worked pair of datasets. On the shortfall of a centre alone: "the mode, median, and mean summarise the data into a single value that is typical or representative of all the values in the dataset, but this is only part of the 'picture' that summarises a dataset." On the two datasets that share all three central measures: "If we just looked at the measures of central tendency, we may assume that the datasets are the same." ### 21. Name which version of the series the number came from: original, seasonally adjusted or trend, which reference period an index sits on, and what a relative unit is relative to.

A time series figure is not one number, it is a choice among several published versions of the same
measurement, and the reader cannot recover which one you used from the value itself. Comparing two
consecutive periods on an original series attributes the calendar's work to the business:
Christmas casuals, the school year, a month with five weekends. Comparing them on a trend series
does the opposite and smooths away a real turning point that has only just happened. Index numbers
carry the same hazard in a different shape, because the level of an index is an artefact of the
reference period chosen for it, so two index numbers on different bases are not comparable even
though both are "the index" and both look like quantities. The discipline is one line of text and
it costs nothing: state the version and the reference period next to the number, every time. What
this prevents is a reader concluding that something changed when what changed was the
transformation. The same hazard reaches any figure whose scale is a property of the instrument
rather than of the world. A full-scale audio reading printed as "dB", a model score printed as a
percentage, a sensor whose floor self-calibrates to its own placement: each is relative to
something the number does not name, so two instruments measuring the same event print two
different figures under one column heading and neither is wrong. The line to write is the same
line: give the scale, and where its calibration comes from, next to the number. And never let an
uncalibrated relative figure be turned into an absolute-sounding word, because a fixed cut-off
applied to a device-relative number converts a property of the equipment into a plain-language
judgement about the world.

*Source: Australian Bureau of Statistics, "Time series data," abs.gov.au/statistics/understanding-statistics/statistical-terms-and-concepts/time-series-data (fetched and read this session; page states "Release date and time" of "02/02/2023 12:00pm AEDT"), which sets out the three published versions and what each one has already had done to it. ## E. Dashboards: designing for a named decision-maker

### 14. Decide whether a dashboard is the right artefact at all, before you design one.

A dashboard is the most expensive thing in this domain to build and by far the most expensive to
keep. It is also the artefact least able to explain anything, because it hands the reader a set of
views and no commentary, which means it transfers the interpretation work from the analyst who
understands the data to the audience who does not. The failure this prevents is a dashboard built
because somebody asked for one, which then needs a permanent owner, goes stale when the questions
change, and quietly becomes the source of confident wrong conclusions nobody is there to correct.
The tests are concrete: is there a real user need, does the data update often enough to justify
automation, will people come back, and do you have the resource to run it. If the data changes
rarely, a report or a set of charts inside a written analysis is cheaper and says more.

*Source: Government Analysis Function, "Data visualisation: building and managing dashboards," analysisfunction.civilservice.gov.uk/policy-store/data-visualisation-building-and-managing-dashboards/ (fetched and read this session; page states "Publication date: 6 February 2026"): "Alternative formats may better address user needs or be a better use of resource, so you must have clear justifications for any proposed dashboard." The conditions given under "Only consider using a dashboard when:" are "there is an identified user need", "data updates are frequent and can be automated, with users likely to revisit the dashboard", "users need flexibility of data combinations and the ability to interact with data" and "you have sufficient resource to support the dashboard". Its stated disadvantages are the argument of this rule: "Dashboards are not good at explaining what’s happening in the data and users may have to discover insights for themselves," and its list of what dashboards do includes "put the burden of understanding data and important messages onto the users", "are not suitable for data that requires a large amount of explanation", "can quickly become obsolete if findings and user needs change", "require resource to maintain and improve", "can overwhelm users" and "can risk the misuse of statistics". On staffing: "Dashboards often require more resource than initially identified due to their complexity. They are not an appropriate project for a single person or for temporary staff." The page also names the cheaper alternatives, "official statistics releases", "standalone data releases", "slide packs" and "visual infographics", and warns "as dashboards do not allow for commentary there is a risk that users may be lead to the wrong conclusions about data." ONS Service Manual, "Dashboards," service-manual.ons.gov.uk/data-visualisation/guidance/dashboards (fetched and read this session; no last-updated date was shown on the page) reaches the same conclusion independently: "Only use a dashboard if you have clear justification for doing so. This should be based on an understanding of your users’ needs," and "In most cases it is better to use charts that provide users with specific insights, and to integrate your charts into your analysis," because dashboards "are not well-suited to highlighting important findings; they put the burden of interpreting and understanding data onto the user." Nielsen Norman Group's definition sets the same expectation of what a dashboard is for: dashboards "are collections of data visualizations, presented in a single-page view that imparts at-a-glance information on which users can act quickly," and "Dashboards are focused on a single task: communicating critical information" (NN/g, "Dashboards: Making Charts and Graphs Easier to Understand," as cited at rule 5, fetched and read this session). Rule id: D16-R14. Volatility: medium (publisher guidance, recently revised). ### 15. Build it around named users' decisions, headline first, and plan its retirement on the day you build it.

The wall of metrics is what you get when nobody asked whose decision the dashboard serves: every
number anybody ever requested, laid out flat, with nothing to say which one matters today. The fix
is not visual, it is a set of questions asked of real users before layout begins, the sharpest of
which is what decisions they will make from it. Layout then follows from the answers: the headline
finding in the most prominent position, detail behind it rather than beside it, and the number of
steps to the data the user came for kept as small as possible. Salience is bought by removing
things at least as much as by adding emphasis. The last part is the one teams skip: a dashboard is
a live service, so somebody owns it, reviews whether its measures still match the decisions, and
turns it off when they no longer do, with warning and an archive rather than a dead link.

*Source: Government Analysis Function, "Data visualisation: building and managing dashboards" (as cited at rule 14, fetched and read this session), whose user-needs section lists the questions to ask, including "How are users going to interact with the dashboard and its data?", "What will users do with the data?", "What decisions will users make based on the dashboard?", "Do they need the story told to them through written commentary?", and "Will users have the time to get insight out of dashboards?" It also instructs "Never create a dashboard in isolation. Speak to people before and during the creation of a dashboard to ensure consistency across government." On retirement: "Retire your dashboard if it is not being maintained or user feedback indicates that it is no longer:" is followed by "useful", "relevant" and "providing value for money", with the notice period "Do this at least six months before retirement" and archiving instructions including "keeping data and metadata available, with a clear note that it is no longer updated and the dates or period the data is associated with." Government Analysis Function, "Data visualisation: testing dashboards for design and accessibility," analysisfunction.civilservice.gov.uk/policy-store/data-visualisation-testing-dashboards-for-design-and-accessibility/ (fetched and read this session; page states "Publication date: 6 February 2026") gives the layout rule: "Design your dashboard using an inverted pyramid structure, with your most important headline content up front and detailed information in other tabs," with "Users should be able to get the dashboard data in as few steps as possible," and the instructions to "minimise the need to scroll on a dashboard page", "limit the number of clicks a user needs to get to the data they want", "avoid horizontal scrolling" and "paginate large tables rather than allowing them to spill off the screen". On clutter it advises "prioritising important metrics and visuals", "using white space effectively", "limiting colour schemes and fonts to a few consistent choices" and "ensuring that any data visualisations are simple and clear". ONS Service Manual, "Dashboards" (as cited at rule 14): "put the most important insights at the top or in the most prominent position"; "you do not have to fit everything onto one screen; if the most important information is visible, the user can scroll up and down"; "regularly review the measures in the dashboard to ensure they meet changing user needs and priorities"; and "use written content to provide context and use accompanying articles to highlight current trends and priorities." ## F. Accessibility and evaluation

### 16. Never let colour be the only thing carrying the meaning, and check the chart in greyscale.

Colour is the encoding that silently excludes people. Around one in twelve men cannot distinguish
some pairs of hues, low-vision users need adjacent colours to differ in brightness and not only in
hue, and a chart that gets printed or photocopied in black and white loses hue entirely. A legend
is the usual offender, because matching a key entry to a line is a colour-only operation by
construction, which is why direct labelling in rule 6 is an accessibility fix as well as a clarity
one. The second offender is the surrounding prose: writing "the green line fell" makes the text
itself colour-dependent, and a screen reader user gets nothing from it. Greyscale is the cheap
test, because a chart that survives it was never relying on colour alone.

*Source: W3C, "Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) 2.2," w3.org/TR/WCAG22/ (fetched and read this session; the document's own status line reads "W3C Recommendation 12 December 2024"), Success Criterion 1.4.1 Use of Color (Level A): "Color is not used as the only visual means of conveying information, indicating an action, prompting a response, or distinguishing a visual element." Success Criterion 1.4.11 Non-text Contrast (Level AA): "The visual presentation of the following have a contrast ratio of at least 3:1 against adjacent color(s)," which for charts is the "Graphical Objects" clause, "Parts of graphics required to understand the content, except when a particular presentation of graphics is essential to the information being conveyed." Success Criterion 1.3.3 Sensory Characteristics (Level A) covers the prose: "Instructions provided for understanding and operating content do not rely solely on sensory characteristics of components such as shape, color, size, visual location, orientation, or sound." Government Analysis Function, "Data visualisation: colours" (as cited at rule 6, fetched and read this session) applies all three to charts specifically. On 1.4.1: "This means we should help users understand charts without relying only on colour. To do this we should avoid using legends in line charts and pie charts. Instead, label the lines and sectors on the chart." On 1.3.3: "This means if you refer to a chart in your text, you should not use colour or shape to talk about the chart. For example you should not refer to ‘the green line’ or ‘the blue bar’," and "It also applies to the use of legends as they rely on users being able to match chart elements using colour." On population: "Colour blindness affects around 8% of men and 0.5% women." On the greyscale test: "It is a good idea to check your charts can be understood in greyscale." The reasons given under "This is because:" are "it is a good way to check if your chart relies on colour to be understood", "there is still a need for some users to print in black and white" and "seeing in greyscale is a specific type of colour blindness". The page publishes a tested categorical palette for reuse, "Dark blue #12436D", "Turquoise #28A197", "Dark pink #801650", "Orange #F46A25", "Dark grey #3D3D3D" and "Light purple #A285D1", to be used "in the order given in the list" so that adjacent colours retain contrast, and recommends dark blue and orange as the two-colour pair "because this is the pair of colours from the palette with the highest contrast ratio." NN/g, "Dashboards" (as cited at rule 5) states the perceptual reason colour should never be the primary category cue: "color properties such as hue or saturation are helpful as a secondary grouping cue, rather than as the main way of showing groups or categories." ### 17. Give every chart a text alternative that serves the same purpose the chart does, and put it where everyone can read it.

A chart is non-text content, so it needs an alternative, and the requirement is not "describe the
image" but "serve the equivalent purpose", which is a different and harder test. The design
decision is what a sighted reader is meant to get. If they are meant to read values off it, the
alternative is the data as an accessible table or download. If they are meant to take away a
finding, the alternative is that finding written out. A complex image needs both halves: a short
description that identifies it, and a long description carrying the information. Two failures are
routine. The first is an alt attribute that restates the title, which serves no purpose at all.
The second is burying the long description somewhere only assistive technology reaches, when the
people who benefit most from a plain-language summary include everyone reading on a phone.

*Source: W3C WAI, "Complex Images," w3.org/WAI/tutorials/images/complex/ (fetched and read this session; page states "Updated 08 April 2026"), which sets the two-part requirement: "Complex images contain substantial information", typically "graphs and charts, including flow charts and organizational charts", and "In these cases, a two-part text alternative is required. The first part is the short description to identify the image and, where appropriate, indicate the location of the long description. The second part is the long description". On placement: "Complex images can be difficult to understand by many people", and "Make long descriptions available to everyone to reach a wider audience with your content." And on the surrounding text: "It is also good practice to refer to and summarize more complex images from the accompanying text." The page also records a technique limitation: an `aria-describedby` long description "is treated as one continuous paragraph of text. Screen readers and other assistive technology do not have access to structural information, such as any headings and tables." The underlying obligation is WCAG 2.2 (as cited at rule 16, fetched and read this session), Success Criterion 1.1.1 Non-text Content (Level A): "All non-text content that is presented to the user has a text alternative that serves the equivalent purpose, except for the situations listed below." Government Analysis Function, "Data visualisation: charts" (as cited at rule 1, fetched and read this session) turns that into the design decision this rule states: for charts the alternative can be "a table of the data presented in the chart" or "a text description of the message the chart is presenting", and "Which you choose should depend on what you would expect a non-disabled user to get from the chart. If you expect a non-disabled user to read the data from the chart, give a table. If you expect them to take away an overall understanding of the data, give a text description of what you want them to understand. If you expect them to do both, give both." On quality: "The text description should describe what you want users to understand from the chart. It should not repeat the chart title, be a literal description or outline every data point shown." On placement: "our advice is that this should live in the body of the page, directly underneath the chart," with the image then marked as decorative. ONS Service Manual, "Chart text" (as cited at rule 10) gives a concrete form: "Use chart alt text to provide a specific description of the type of chart and the main trend that it is showing. ### 18. Test the artefact on someone who did not build it, and ask what they concluded, not whether they liked it.

You cannot see your own chart, because you already know the answer and your eye goes straight to
it. The only reliable check is to hand it to somebody who does not know the data, say nothing, and
ask them what it tells them. The failure this catches is the chart that is clear to its author and
ambiguous to everybody else, which otherwise surfaces in a meeting where a decision is taken on a
misreading. Two disciplines make the test worth running. Ask for their interpretation, not their
opinion of the design, because an opinion is cheap and unrelated to whether the chart worked. And
do not use colleagues who worked on the same data, since they share your knowledge and will read
the chart correctly for the wrong reason. The last check closes the loop the whole domain exists
for: find out whether the artefact actually informed the decision, or whether the decision was
made some other way and the chart was decoration.

*Source: NN/g, "Contrast" (as cited at rule 10, fetched and read this session): "The easiest way to know if people will understand your charts is to test them, just like you would with any other design. Show a member of your intended audience (team member or stakeholder) your data visualization and ask them to summarize their interpretation," with the remedial loop "If the chart is unclear, return to the 3Cs and consider what changes might be needed: more context and contrast, or less clutter?" ONS Service Manual, "Principles" (as cited at rule 1, fetched and read this session) states the same test and names the reason: "It is often easier to understand a chart when you are already familiar with the data. Your chart should make the data understandable to a non-expert user. Show your chart to someone less familiar with the data to see if they can understand the trend you are trying to show." Government Analysis Function, "Data visualisation: charts" (as cited at rule 1) makes testing an instruction rather than an option, "We advise to test your charts with users to see if they are working as you planned," and supplies the independence constraint this rule turns on: "avoid picking colleagues that work or have worked on your data". For dashboards specifically, Analysis Function, "Data visualisation: testing dashboards" (as cited at rule 15) requires ongoing evaluation rather than a one-off check, and the building-and-managing page requires teams to "regularly get feedback through user research to understand how your service is meeting user needs" and to "actively monitor usage analytics and success metrics to assess the service and its future." The final instruction in this rule, to check whether the artefact actually informed the decision rather than merely being present at it, is common practice; checked the NN/g contrast article, the ONS principles page and both Analysis Function dashboard pages, 2026-08-06, and none states it. The nearest sourced approximation is the Analysis Function's list of questions to ask users before building, which includes "What decisions will users make based on the dashboard?" Rule id: D16-R18. Volatility: durable. Verified: 2026-08-06 (current, primary source, for the NN/g article; current, publisher documentation, for the ONS and Analysis Function guidance).*

## Known gaps

Stated per METHOD.md: a stated gap is a risk, an unstated one is a surprise. These are subjects
this rule set does not currently cover, not claims that they do not matter.

- **Data acquisition and cleaning are uncovered by this framework entirely.** Every rule above
  assumes the numbers are already right. Provenance, collection bias, missingness, deduplication,
  outlier handling, unit and timezone errors, and the question of whether the dataset can answer
  the question at all sit upstream of presentation and are not covered here, in domain 01, or
  anywhere else. This is the largest single gap adjacent to this domain, because a beautifully
  presented wrong number is more dangerous than an ugly one.
- **Choosing a summary statistic is now partly covered, and the remainder is named.** The
  2026-08-07 research cycle closed most of this gap from national statistical office
  documentation: rule 19 covers mean against median under skew and outliers, rule 20 covers
  spread, percentiles and when to show the distribution instead of a central value, and rule 21
  covers seasonally adjusted and trend series and index reference periods. Three things named in
  the original gap are still open. What a sample-against-population caveat should actually say in
  a chart caption or a dashboard tile is not covered: rules 9 and 13 require uncertainty and the
  word "estimate", and the ABS census-and-sample page confirms that "as data are collected from a
  subset of units and inferences made about the whole population, the data are subject to
  'sampling' error", but no fetched source gives the wording or placement of the caveat itself.
  Base effects, meaning the arithmetic by which an unusual comparison period distorts a
  year-on-year rate, are not covered; rule 21 covers which series version you are on and not this.
  And rates and ratios whose denominator moves, where the headline can invert depending on whether
  the numerator or the denominator did the work, are absent entirely. The 2026-08-09 cycle closed
  the adjacent but narrower case: rule 13 now requires the denominator to be printed beside a share
  and a floor set below which no percentage is shown. It sets no number, because neither held
  reference text sets one and the only floor in evidence is one project's judgement about one
  decision; what a defensible floor should be, and how it should vary with the decision, is
  unaddressed here.
- **The primary data-visualisation literature is only partly present.** As first written on
  2026-08-06 this gap covered every book and paper in the field, none of which had been read. Two
  are now held and read: Few's *Show Me the Numbers*, 2nd edition, first quoted on 2026-08-08 and
  quoted again on 2026-08-09 in rules 8, 13 and 21, and Nussbaumer Knaflic's *Storytelling with
  Data*, first quoted on 2026-08-09 in rules 8, 9, 13 and 21. Both are cited from held copies with
  page numbers and string-matched extractions. The rest of the gap stands unchanged: Tufte's books,
  Cleveland and McGill's 1985 graphical-perception paper, Schwabish's books and Pandey et al.'s
  2015 deception study have not been read, and where they appear above they appear only as quoted
  or cited by a page that was fetched, with the citation to that page. Rule 5's perceptual claims
  therefore still rest on NN/g's account of the underlying research rather than on the research.
  Four claims were searched in the two held books on 2026-08-09 and are recorded as
  checked-negative rather than unsearched: a minimum denominator before a share may be shown
  (neither book sets a floor), uncertainty computed upstream and dropped before the reader sees it
  (neither book uses the word uncertainty at all), a finding sentence hard-coded into a report
  generator (neither book addresses generated artefacts), and the calibration provenance of a
  device-relative unit (neither book mentions calibration). Those four clauses stand on this
  register's own proving run and are labelled as such in their rules.
- **Statistical inference beyond the two rules in section D.** Confidence-interval construction,
  multiple comparisons, base rates, Simpson's paradox, regression to the mean and survivorship
  bias are all mechanisms by which an honest chart supports a wrong decision, and none is covered.
- **Interactive and exploratory visualisation.** Everything above is aimed at explanatory
  artefacts. Filtering, brushing, drill-down interaction models, tooltips and the accessibility of
  interactive charts are only touched at the edges by the dashboard rules. The Analysis Function's
  dashboard-testing guidance names real problems here, including that charts made with some
  libraries "cannot be navigated with a keyboard", and this document does not develop them.
- **Maps and geographic presentation.** Choropleth classification, the effect of area size on
  perceived magnitude, projection choice and rate-versus-count mapping are entirely absent. Rule 4
  says spatial relationships go to a map and stops there.
- **Real-time and operational displays.** Alerting thresholds, refresh cadence, what a display
  should do when data is stale or missing, and the design of a screen somebody watches rather than
  reads are not covered. Rule 14's distinction between operational and analytical dashboards is
  drawn from NN/g and not developed.
- **Australian obligations.** The accessibility rules above are sourced to W3C and to UK
  publishers whose legal framing is the Public Sector Bodies (Websites and Mobile Applications)
  Accessibility Regulations 2018 and the Equality Act 2010. No Australian source was fetched and
  nothing here should be read as a statement of Australian law.
- **Print, slide and email as delivery contexts.** All the accessibility guidance above is
  web-oriented. Charts pasted into slide decks, PDFs and emails lose their text alternatives and
  their data downloads in ways this rule set does not address.
- **Numeracy and audience.** How much statistical literacy to assume, how to present the same
  finding to a technical and a non-technical reader, and whether to present a range to an audience
  that will read it as a point estimate anyway, are all unaddressed.
